Mathematics of Football

How Can We Represent Football With Vectors?

Before a computer can analyze a football play, we first have to turn what happens on the field into numbers. Vectors give us one of the simplest ways to do that.

Start With a Player's Position

Imagine looking straight down at a football field. Every player is standing somewhere along the length and width of the field.

We can describe a player's location with two numbers. For example:

p = [30, 20]

The first number can represent the player's position along the length of the field, while the second represents the player's position across the width.

Together, these numbers form a vector.

Player positions can be represented using coordinates. The arrow shows the change in position from Player A to Player B.

Vectors Can Describe Movement

Suppose Player A begins at (30, 20) and later moves to (45, 30).

We can calculate the change in position by subtracting the starting position from the ending position:

[45, 30] − [30, 20] = [15, 10]

The vector [15, 10] describes how the player's position changed.

This is a displacement vector.

Instead of only knowing where a player is, we can now begin mathematically describing how that player moves.

A Player Can Have More Than Two Features

Position is only the beginning. Modern football tracking data can record several measurements about a player at once.

A simplified player vector might look like:

x = [position x, position y, speed, direction]

Instead of two numbers, we now have four.

We can think of this player as being represented in a four-dimensional feature space.

A computer does not need to visualize four dimensions the way we visualize a football field. It can still perform mathematical operations on vectors containing many features.

Does Every Feature Give Us New Information?

Imagine that our player vector includes both:

  • speed in miles per hour
  • speed in kilometers per hour

These appear to be two different measurements, but one can be calculated directly from the other:

speedkm/h ≈ 1.609 × speedmph

That means the second feature does not give us a completely new piece of information.

This connects to the idea of linear dependence: some information in a dataset may be mathematically redundant.

From Vectors to Football Analytics

Representing football with vectors gives us a mathematical language for describing what happens on the field.

Once players and plays become numerical representations, we can eventually ask questions such as:

Which players are moving in similar ways?

Which football plays are mathematically similar?

Are there repeated patterns across thousands of plays?

Can complicated plays be represented using fewer meaningful dimensions?

Those questions eventually lead into statistics and machine learning. But the first step is simply learning how to turn football into vectors.
